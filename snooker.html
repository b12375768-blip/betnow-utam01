Snooker: the table, the balls and the rules

Snooker looks complicated from the outside and it is not. Every frame follows the same shape: red, then colour, until the reds are gone. This page explains the shape of the game, the exact specifications of the tables we play on, and how fouls are penalised.

The table we play on

A full-size snooker table is a metre and a half longer than a pool table, and the size is fixed by the rules. That is why a snooker table feels slower: the balls have further to travel.

Standard snooker table specification
Playing area3569 mm × 1778 mm (12 ft × 6 ft)
Baulk line737 mm from the baulk cushion

PocketsSix; corner pockets about 85 mm, middle pockets slightly wider
Ball diameter52.5 mm
ClothWorsted wool cloth with a directional nap
CushionsReversible K66 profile, nose height about 35 mm

The nap is the direction the cloth fibres lie. A ball rolling with the nap is quicker and truer; against the nap it is slower and can drift. Ironing or brushing the cloth the wrong way is the most common reason a good table plays badly, which is why we cover it in the table care guide.

Ball in hand is played from inside the D, between the baulk cushions, with both feet on the floor. It is not a hand that picks the ball up: once you touch the cue ball anywhere on the table, you are committing to a shot.

The 22 balls and what they score

Fifteen reds and six colours make 21 balls, plus the white cue ball that never scores. The colours stay on the table throughout the frame and are re-spotted whenever a red is potted, which is why a single red can be worth eight points if you follow it with the black.

The highest break in a standard frame is 147: fifteen reds with a black after each, then all six colours for 27. It is a mathematical ceiling, not something we advertise or charge towards.

How a frame is played

  1. Break offEvery frame starts from behind the baulk line. You must hit the red nearest the corner cushion with the cue ball and, in practice, contact it as full as you can manage without potting it.
  2. Red, then a colourWhile reds remain, the ball on is always a red. After potting one you nominate a colour and pot it. Any colour is allowed as your "colour" at this stage, and you may nominate the same one several frames in a row.
  3. Colours are re-spottedYour colour goes back on its spot while any red remains. So the pattern repeats: red, colour, red, colour.
  4. The last redPot the final red and play your colour again. This colour is not re-spotted, because no red remains.
  5. Colours in sequenceYellow, green, brown, blue, pink, black. The first potted colour that is not the next in sequence loses the frame, so the order has to be respected.
  6. Frame endsThe frame is over when the black is potted, when a player concedes, or when a foul ends the frame. The score is only written down after both players are satisfied.

Fouls and penalties

A foul is punished by handing the opponent points. The penalty is the value of the ball on, or the value of the ball involved in the foul, whichever is higher, with a minimum of 4 and a maximum of 7.

Pushing or double-hitting the cue ballValue of the highest ball involved
Touching a ball with your handAs if that ball had been potted

After most fouls the incoming player chooses where to play from. After an in-off the cue ball is placed in the baulk area, which can be a gift: it puts you back on the yellow, green or brown.

Free ball and the miss rule

These two rules exist for the same reason — to stop a player deliberately parking the cue ball where nothing can be potted.

  • Free ball. If the cue ball is snookered on every ball that is on, you may nominate any other ball as the ball on. You still have to pot a ball first, and you do not score as if it were the colour you nominated.
  • Miss rule. After a foul, your opponent can ask the referee to require you to play again from the same position. If you fail to hit the ball on and pot it again, the frame can be awarded to your opponent.

We play by the World Snooker rules, and the current rules card is fixed to the wall next to the league tables. If you disagree with a ruling, ask the referee before the next shot is played.

Position play for beginners

The score follows the pot, but the frame is won by where the cue ball finishes. These three habits carry new players a long way.

Think in balls, not points

A red is one point; the black that follows it is seven. Players who only chase reds fall behind because they are always one colour behind where they could have been.

Keep the cue ball safe

When the table is tight, a simple roll-and-run to a safety is worth more than a risky pot. Snooker is decided by who forces the other player to guess.

Learn one break shot

One repeatable break-off beats five experimental ones. Build it, film it if you can, and use it every frame until it is automatic.

On our tables

Playing snooker here

  • Snooker tables are played on a first-come basis unless a table is booked, so check with the counter before you set up.
  • Cues and chalk can be borrowed. We ask for a deposit on borrowed equipment, returned with the cue.
  • League tables are marked on the cloth edge. Play on those only when a referee tells you to.
  • Mark your score on the board at the table and confirm it with your opponent before the next frame starts.
