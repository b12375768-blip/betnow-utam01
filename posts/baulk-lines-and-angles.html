Baulk lines, angles and the break shot in snooker

The first shot of every frame decides who touches the table first, and it is the one shot you can practise until it is automatic. It needs no score and no flair — just the same contact, in the same place, with the same speed.

Snooker technique

8 minute readWritten by our snooker coaching teamUpdated 1 October 2026

The break-off must contact the red nearest the corner cushion, from behind the baulk line. Everything else about the shot is your choice: how thin, how hard, and where you want the cue ball to finish. Get those three decisions right and you have a shot you can repeat for twenty years.

What the break-off is actually for

Three jobs, in order of importance:

  1. Do not foul. The break-off red has to be hit first.
  2. Do not give your opponent an easy pot, an easy safety, or worse, an easy snooker.
  3. Ideally, leave yourself a look at a colour or a thin red so the frame does not become a pure safety battle.

Beginners forget the first two and chase the third. Open the table and hand over a black, and you are behind within three shots.

Why a full hit on the corner red fails

Hitting the corner red as full as possible feels powerful and looks confident. It is the most common break-off mistake for a simple reason: the cue ball and the red go in opposite directions. Contact the red dead full and the white runs almost straight back down the table, into the pack you have just disturbed, or into a middle pocket.

The result is predictable: the pack jumps, the cue ball returns to baulk, and your opponent walks into a table of reds and colours. You have given away the frame for the price of one shot.

Where to aim instead

Aim at the outer edge of the pack — the thin sliver of the corner red that faces the side cushion — and cut it rather than driving into it. The aim is:

  • The red goes towards the side cushion and away from you.
  • The cue ball goes up the table, ideally onto the side cushion, and comes back to a position where you can see reds and at least one colour.

In practice that means a cut of roughly a quarter to a third of the ball on the corner red. A quarter-ball contact feels thin enough to frighten you and is about right. If you are cutting more than a third, your cue ball will run too far up the table and your opponent will have a straightforward clearance.

One adjustment makes it much safer.Use a touch of outside (running) side on the cue ball. The spin pushes the cue ball off the cushion line and away from the pack, which both reduces the chance of a scratch and shortens the white’s path back down the table.

How much power

Enough to open the table, not enough to jump balls or send them over cushions.

  • Too soft:the pack barely moves, one red drops into a pocket and the table stays closed.
  • Correct:the pack spreads two or three reds wide, no ball leaps, and the cue ball returns along the cushion.
  • Too hard:balls bounce over cushions or off the table, which is a foul and an unnecessary gift to your opponent.

If you can hear the reds hitting each other hard, you are using too much. A frame break-off that a spectator cannot hear is usually about right.

Six steps to a repeatable break-off

  1. Place the cue ballPut it in the middle of the D or just behind the brown, well clear of the side cushions. Starting wide is the most common cause of a bad break-off.
  2. Walk to the shotStand up, look at the pack, then find the cue ball, the red and the cushion as a straight line. Aim at the edge of the red, not at its middle.
  3. Chalk in handChalk while you walk, with the cube in your bridge hand, then put it down. Never chalk after you are down on the shot.
  4. Set the bridgeHand flat on the cloth, index finger along the cue, about a fist’s width from the ball. Cue level, just under the elbow or through the bridge.
  5. Practise the stroke twiceSlow, straight, no balls. The last practice stroke should look exactly like the real one.
  6. Deliver and follow throughPause under the chin for a beat, then stroke. Head still, elbow still, and let the cue follow through to where your forearm finishes.

How to practise it

One session is enough to build the habit properly:

  • Ask the referee or a member to re-rack for you, then take ten break-offs in a row.
  • Count your results out of ten: no foul, no scratch, and table left open.
  • Note one thing each time. Only change one variable at a time — aim point, then power, then spin — so you know what fixed the problem.
  • Film two or three from behind the baulk cushion. You will immediately see cue ball drift or the head moving down on the shot.

Common mistakes

Break-off faults and fixes
MistakeWhy it happensFix
Scratch in a side or middle pocketCue ball placed too close to the baulk cushion, or a full hit on the redMove into the middle of the D; aim a quarter-ball cut, not a full hit
Pack jumps, table stays awkwardToo much power with a full hitCut the red, and cut the speed by about half
Cue ball finishes in the packAim too thin, or the head moved down during the strokeAim slightly fuller, then add running side to steer the white away
Miscue on the cutSide applied but the tip not hitting the cue ball cleanlyReduce the side until it is reliable, then add it back
Foul by hitting the wrong redAim not checked under the cueGet down and look before setting the bridge, not after

Then what?

After the break-off, the frame is not over. Players win from the awkward table their opponent leaves them far more often than from a clear one. If you have a thin red and a colour, take them. If you have nothing and your opponent has a straightforward red, play a safe and go again. The break-off is one shot; the frame is forty of them.
