Common fouls in English pool and how to avoid them

Most frames lost in a club league are not lost on a missed pot. They are lost on a foul that gave away the table at the wrong moment. Here is what counts as a foul on our tables and how to stop making them.

English pool

6 minute readWritten by our pool coachesUpdated 1 October 2026

We play English 8-ball on the 9 ft tables, which follow the rules card fixed to the table. These rules are stricter than some other pool rule sets in one important way: you must hit the ball you are on cleanly, and potting any other ball is a foul even if your ball goes down. Knowing that one rule prevents most of the surprises beginners meet for the first time.

What is a foul

  • Failing to hit a ball on, or hitting a ball not on before the ball on.
  • Pocketing any ball other than the ball on.
  • Pocketing the cue ball, known as a scratch.
  • Driving the cue ball off the table, or jumping a ball off the table.
  • Touching a ball with your hand, or failing to nominate your ball when the rules require it.
  • Cueing, touching or leaning on the cue ball with anything other than the tip.

Every foul gives your opponent the table and, in 8-ball, ball in hand anywhere on the table. Two of these are worth the most attention.

1. Cutting too thin

A quarter-ball cut is about as thin as you want a long pot to be. Cut it thinner and two things happen at once: the cue ball starts to miss the object ball more often, and the miss becomes a foul rather than just a lost pot.

The habits that fix it:

  • Stand further round the shot than you think you need to. The angle looks steeper than it is from behind the cue; it is not.
  • Use a thinner cut only when you have a genuinely open table and a good reason.
  • If you are unsure, play the safe shot. A foul is always worse than a safe.
  • Practise cut shots at a slow pace. Soft strokes exaggerate errors in cue ball position; firm strokes disguise them.

2. Potting a ball not on

Many players learn this one the hard way: they are on the 3, aiming at a corner, and accidentally drop the 5 into a side pocket. In English 8-ball that is a foul, and the 5 is spotted.

Prevention is mostly visual discipline:

  • Before you shoot, name the ball and the pocket out loud. Saying “three into the bottom right” removes most accidents.
  • Look along the line to the pocket and check what else sits near that pocket.
  • Expecting a double is different from allowing one. Aim at your ball first; a second ball going down is a pleasant surprise, not a plan.

Scratches and how to recover

A scratch is a foul, and your opponent gets ball in hand. The next shot is the most important shot of the frame, so the recovery matters as much as the mistake.

  1. Think before you move.Ball in hand means you can place the cue ball anywhere. Most players put it back exactly where it scratched from, which hands the same shot straight back.
  2. Hide the cue ball.Place it tight behind a ball or a cushion so your opponent has to play a difficult shot or take a risk.
  3. Cover the easy pocket.Position the cue ball so the side pocket your opponent would normally use for the ball on is no longer available.
  4. Do not overthink it.Defensive play in a hurry is usually worse than a simple safe. A simple safe is a perfectly good outcome.

Frozen balls and the ball on the cushion

If your ball on is frozen to another ball, or tight on the cushion, you may play at it as softly as you like. What you may not do is hit the other ball first and then the ball on — that is a foul. This is why a very gentle tap is a legitimate shot and not a trick: the cue ball is not moving far enough to reach anything else.

For the same reason, a deliberate kick off another ball to move the frozen ball is a foul in English pool, even though it looks desperate and even though your opponent would probably think it was fine. If your ball on is frozen, tap it.

Jumping and kicking

Players who have learned jump shots sometimes jump on shots where a normal shot would do. On a 9 ft table, an unnecessary jump is a foul, because the ball leaves the cloth. The same applies to any shot that lifts the object ball off the table.

Use the ball you have. Getting a roll shot onto the correct ball is nearly always better than attempting a jump.

Making the 8

The two ways to lose a frame with the 8 are:

  • Potting it early,for example when you are clearing your last group ball. It must stay on the table until your group is gone.
  • Scratching on the 8.If the cue ball goes in any pocket while you pot the 8, the frame is lost regardless of where the 8 went.

Both are shot decision errors rather than potting errors. On the 8, play the pot you would be happy to hit ten times in a row, then draw the cue ball back off the object ball.

Club etiquette that also protects your frame

  • Confirm the score with your opponent before you start the next frame. Score arguments are the most common disruption in a club league.
  • Clean up after your group of balls and reset the rack if the table needs it. Tell the referee if the balls are not reset correctly.
  • If you disagree with a foul call, raise it before the next shot. A frame settled by argument is a frame lost by both players.

A short checklist before every shot

  1. Name your ball and your pocket.Out loud, every time.
  2. Check the line for a blocker.Anything between the cue ball and the object ball is a foul.
  3. Know where the cue ball goes.One object ball, one plan for position.
  4. Consider the safe.If the pot is doubtful, play safe and keep the initiative.
  5. On the 8,only take the pot you would take with the frame already won.
