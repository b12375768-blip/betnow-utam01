Table etiquette that makes club nights work

Eight tables, two hundred frames and a hundred people in one room only work because of a small set of unwritten habits. None of them are difficult. All of them matter.

Club play

5 minute readWritten by our refereesUpdated 1 October 2026

On a quiet afternoon, nobody notices etiquette. On a Friday evening, it is the difference between a room where people want to be and a room where people queue to leave. These are the habits we ask our members for, and the ones our referees enforce when a frame is being played.

Before you play

  • Check the scoreboard.If your name is on a league draw, that frame is yours whether or not you feel ready. Tell the referee if you need to drop out.
  • Find out the game.Ask which rules are being played on a table before you sit down: English 8-ball, 9-ball or straight pool. Ten minutes of explaining rules is better than a disputed frame.
  • Borrow, do not take.Cues, chalk and rests from the counter are lent to you. Ask, and return them to the counter afterwards.
  • Flat shoes.Trainers are fine. Shoes that mark the floor are not.

While you play

  • Chalk on the rail.Chalk marks on the cloth need work to remove and the rail is exactly where chalk belongs.
  • Stay still during the shot.Walking behind the table, shifting your weight or talking can put a player off. If you must move, walk quietly.
  • Mark where you replace a ball.A small piece of paper or a coin on the cloth saves the argument about which spot the black came off.
  • Say what you are doing to the referee, not across the room.Most disagreements are about what was intended, not what happened.
  • No phone calls on the floor.Take calls outside. A ringing phone during a frame feels much worse than it does to you.
  • Players under 16 stay with an adultwho remains on the premises.

Scoring

Score arguments are the single biggest cause of friction in a club league, and they are almost always avoidable.

  • Announce the score as you pot, not at the end of the frame.
  • Both players confirm the score before the next frame begins. If you disagree, go back to the count now, not in twenty minutes.
  • Write the score on the board or slate at the table, where the referee can see it.
  • In league play, the referee's ruling on the score is final for that frame.

Between frames

  • Re-spot colours.Put the colours back on their spots before you walk away, even if you were only watching.
  • Re-rack when the balls are scattered.If you are the last off the table, reset the balls. It takes fifteen seconds and it makes the next frame start on time.
  • Clear your pocket.Take your chalk, rests and case off the rail before leaving.
  • Report problems.A cloth that has lifted, a pocket that rattles, a cushion that feels dead. Tell staff before the next league night, not after.

When you are stuck behind a frame

Waiting is the part nobody warns you about. A safety battle on a table next to the door can take forty minutes, and you did not pay for a lesson in cue ball control from a table you are not on.

  • Play at the quiet end.Table choice is usually yours, and a table near the corner is easier to watch from.
  • Use the waiting time.Watch the frame in front of you. Being a regular on a quiet club floor is how most players learn to read a safety battle.
  • Ask the players something.“What were you trying to do there?” is welcome at most clubs and turns waiting into a lesson.
  • Move if it is your booking and the game is at a standstill.Agree it with both players first.
  • Do not stand over the table doing commentary.It slows the game down for everyone.

If you are new

  • Nobody at a club floor expects you to know the rules on day one. Ask the referee; it is their job.
  • Be honest about your level. Being honest gets you into the right league and better games, which is more fun than a league you win two frames in.
  • Turn up ten minutes early. The first ten minutes of a club evening is when other players are most willing to make time for you.
  • Thank the people you played with. That is the whole social contract of a club.

If someone breaks these rules

Tell a member of staff, not the whole room. Referees deal with it, and that is why they are on the floor. If a game starts to involve money — a side bet, an informal pot — a referee will end it, and that rule is not negotiable. See our terms and conditions.
