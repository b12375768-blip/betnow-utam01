A pre-shot routine that survives a tight frame

The shots you miss are rarely the ones you cannot execute. They are the ones you played in a hurry, at the wrong time, without deciding what the shot was for. A routine fixes that.

Match routine

5 minute readWritten by our coaching teamUpdated 1 October 2026

A routine is not a ritual for looking serious. It is a checklist that makes sure the same things happen before every shot, including the twenty-fifth shot when you are tired and behind.

The six steps

  1. Read the table.Stand up and look at the whole table, not at the ball you are aiming at. Where are the reds, which colours can be reached, and what does your opponent get if this shot goes wrong?
  2. Name the shot.Say to yourself what this is: “pot the red and come off two cushions for the black”, or “safety to baulk”. A shot with a name is a shot you chose.
  3. Decide the finish.Before you go down, know exactly where the cue ball has to stop. If you cannot name the spot, you are not aiming, you are hoping.
  4. Check your line and the rules.Cue ball, object ball, pocket, all in one line with nothing in the way. In pool, name the ball and the pocket out loud. It takes a second and prevents the foul that gives away the frame.
  5. Chalk and set up.Chalk in your bridge hand while you walk to the table. Set the bridge, get the cue level, and take two slow practice strokes that look exactly like the real one.
  6. Commit.Pause. Look one last time along the line. Breathe out, stroke, follow through, and stay down until the ball has travelled a foot or so. The shot is over when your head comes up.

Why the pause matters

Step six is the one players drop first and miss most. Without a pause, the cue ball is struck while the head is still moving and the aim has already shifted. A single second of stillness is worth more than any cue upgrade.

Give the pause a physical cue so it happens on cue: the last practice stroke ending, the tip touching the ball, a breath out. Same cue, every shot.

Adapting the routine to the situation

  • When leading by a lot.Keep the routine, but remove the unnecessary risk. Simple shots, no side, no jumping. Do not give a lead away by trying to be impressive.
  • When behind.Keep the routine exactly as it is, and add two things: choose shots that create the next shot, and remember that a safety is a legitimate shot. Most frames lost from behind are lost by forcing a pot.
  • On the last ball of a frame.Slow down physically. Breathe out longer before the stroke. The black is potted, not stroked.
  • When time is short.Cut steps four and five, not step six. Never cut the pause and the look.

When the routine breaks

It will be interrupted: an opponent concedes, a ball goes in the jaws, someone knocks on the door, or a league table finishes late and the next frame starts immediately. What to do next is what separates a routine from a superstition.

Recovering from an interruption
SituationWhat to do
Opponent concedes mid-frameDo not walk off. Stay at the table and complete the routine on the next shot; conceding players often come back the following week.
You miscue or nick the ballTake one full breath, look at the table again from the other end if you can, and reset. Do not immediately re-play the same stroke out of frustration.
Your opponent is playing fast and noisySlow your own routine down rather than speeding up. Your routine is the thing keeping your score together.
You cannot remember what you were going to doWalk to the other end of the table and look back. The answer is almost always obvious from the other side.
Two frames lost in a rowChange the pace, not the aim. Play one frame deliberately slowly. Winning one frame stops the spiral better than any technical change.

Building it so it sticks

  • Use the same six steps in practice and in matches. A routine used only under pressure does not exist.
  • Keep it short enough that you are not bored. Thirty seconds is plenty.
  • Write it on a small card and keep it in your cue case until it becomes automatic.
  • Coach one thing at a time. Once step five is habit, tighten step three.
  • Ask your opponent what they see. Watching a frame on a phone from behind the table shows you head movement immediately.

What good looks like

A player with a good routine appears unhurried. They look at the table more than once, they pause before every stroke, and their mistakes come in twos rather than in runs. Opponents find it hard to rattle, because there is nothing to rattle: the process does not change when the score does.
