Your first 100-point frame: a simple plan

A hundred points is not a milestone, it is a rough average for a developing player. Here is a plan that gets you there without gambling your frame on one difficult pot.

Beginner snooker

6 minute readWritten by our coaching teamUpdated 1 October 2026

New players tend to lose frames in three ways: potting the first easy red and then having no plan for the next shot, taking the colour when no colour is available, and playing a pot when a safety was the better option. None of those mistakes require skill to avoid. They require a plan.

The arithmetic that matters

While reds remain, a red is worth 1 and any colour is worth between 2 and 7. So:

  • Red and black, repeated, is 8 points a time.
  • Red and blue, repeated, is 6 points a time.
  • Red and brown is 5 points a time.
  • Clearing fifteen reds and then the colours is 147 — but only with the right position.

This is why the colour you choose matters more than the red you potted. A red followed by the yellow is a third of the score of the same red followed by the black.

The plan

  1. Count the reds firstBefore the break-off, look at the table and count the reds you can see. Beginners usually underestimate by half. Once you know the number, you know which score is realistic.
  2. Break off and switch offDo not stand over the table for two minutes watching. Hit your break-off, walk to the other end, and look at the table from there. The view from the opposite end is usually much better than the one you had.
  3. Take the easy red, not the difficult oneIf there is a red you would pot for certain, pot it. If the only reds available are difficult and your opponent has an easy one, play safe and go again.
  4. Look for a colour before you pot the redWhere the white will finish determines whether the red is worth anything. A red that leaves you nowhere is worth 1 point and a turn.
  5. Choose the colour you can actually reachAfter the red, choose based on the position, not on greed. Brown or blue is fine. Black is for when the white is going past it with room to spare.
  6. Do not pot a red into a clusterPutting a red on top of three others hands your opponent an easy clearance. Pot reds that open the table.
  7. Finish the frame in orderWhen the last red is gone, the colours are potted in sequence: yellow, green, brown, blue, pink, black. Do not attempt the black until you are comfortable — a frame lost on a rushed black is the most common way beginners lose a frame they were winning.

When to play safe

Play safe when any of these is true:

  • There is no red on the table that you are genuinely confident of potting.
  • You cannot see a colour after potting the red.
  • The white is not going anywhere, so the pot is not available at all.
  • Your opponent has a comfortable red and you have nothing.

A simple safety that works at every level: hit the cue ball lightly into the pack so it comes back to baulk, leaving your opponent to play from the D. Better still, get the cue ball behind a colour so your opponent cannot see the pack at all.

Position rules that keep score moving

  • When the white is under the D,the black is usually the best colour — it is big, it is aimed at from a predictable line, and it puts you near the reds.
  • When you are near the blue,the pink is often the best option. It is a big target and it leads to the black.
  • When the white is tight to a cushion,do not try to spin it away. Come off the cushion and play the shot you have.
  • When you have a choice between two reds,take the one that leaves the white in baulk with a colour available. That is the shot that builds a hundred.

A worked example

You have broken off and come back to the table with ten reds left and a comfortable red to the black. Here is a realistic scoring route:

  • Red, black, red, black: 16 points and four shots.
  • No red available on the black: play a safety rather than forcing a thin cut.
  • Next visit: red, pink (6), red, brown (5) if the black is not on: 13 more points.
  • Continue until the reds are gone, then take the colours in order from wherever the white finishes: 27 points.

That is a hundred points built on four or five pots, not on heroics. Notice that the black is used when it is easy and dropped when it is not. Consistency in choosing is worth more than consistency in potting.

Scorekeeping

Two habits that make frames unwinnable without them:

  • Count the reds out loud as you pot them.It stops you re-racking a frame you have already won.
  • Confirm the score with your opponent before the next frame starts.It is faster than arguing about it later.

What to work on next

Once the plan is automatic, the next improvement is controlling where the white finishes, because position is what turns a run of easy reds into a large score. Practise the break-off in between so every frame starts the same way.
