Cues, chalk, tips and looking after a table

You do not need an expensive cue to play well, and you do need a few simple habits to keep whatever cue you own in working order. This page covers what actually changes your results, what is only cosmetic, and how we look after the cloth and cushions.

Choosing a cue

Four things decide how a cue feels: length, weight, balance point and tip hardness. Everything else is decoration. If you are buying your first cue, spend on these four and keep the rest of the budget in your pocket.

What matters when you buy a cue
FeatureWhat to look for
LengthStandard snooker cues are about 147 cm; pool cues about 147 cm (58 in) are also normal. Length mostly affects reach, not quality.
WeightBetween roughly 540 g and 600 g is the usual range. Heavier cues feel steadier, lighter cues feel easier on a weak stroke.
Balance pointJust forward of centre feels neutral. A very front-heavy cue suits slow, deliberate players.
Tip hardnessSoft tips grip but mark the ball; hard tips last but miscue more. Mid-range tips are the sensible starting point.
Tip diameterAbout 9.5 mm for snooker and 12.5–13 mm for pool. Match the tip to the game.
FerruleIvory-coloured and firm; it is what actually strikes the ball.
ShaftStraightness matters more than the wood species. Check that it is straight in the case before you commit.

What is not worth paying for

  • Inlays and fancy wraps. They do not change how the cue plays.
  • Weights under the butt cap. Useful only if a cue is too light for your stroke.
  • Low-deflection shafts at beginner level. Worth considering once you can already keep the cue ball in a small area.
  • Extra-long or articulated cues. Buy these when you need reach, not as a default.
Bring your cue in. If you already own a cue, drop it at the counter. We will look at the tip, the ferrule and the joints and tell you what actually needs attention, even if that answer is “nothing”.

Chalk, tips and miscues

A miscue is not bad luck. It is the tip telling you that it has run out of grip, or that your tip is not hitting the centre of the ball.

Chalk correctly

  • Keep the cube in your bridge hand, close to the tip.
  • Roll the chalk around the tip as you walk to the table.
  • Press the chalk on with a twist, not a scrub, then brush the excess off against the rail.
  • Put the chalk down before you address the shot. Chalking while aiming is how people chalk the cue.

Hit the centre

Side spin only works if the tip contacts the cue ball cleanly off-centre, roughly between the centre and a quarter of the ball's radius. Straying further out either miscues or produces far less spin than players expect.

Know when to re-tip

Replace the tip when it is shorter than a third of the original diameter, when the edges are crumbling, or when the ball starts skidding on contact. A mushroomed tip scuffs the ball and marks the cloth.

Keeping your cue in good condition

Five minutes of care after a session keeps a cue playable for years.

  • Wipe the tip and ferruleChalk dust that stays on the ferrule will scratch the ball and can work into the tip edge.
  • Do not drag the butt on the floorThe joint and the wrap take the damage long before the tip does.
  • Keep the cue level on the floorStand it against a wall or in a case, never leaning where it can be knocked over.
  • Do not leave it in a hot carHeat and dry air are the fastest way to damage a shaft's glue line.
  • Check the joint now and thenA quarter turn of play is a sign to tighten it, not to play on with it.
  • Replace the ferrule before the leather goesA leather ferrule sits on the shaft and is cheap to change. Cracked glue is not.

Looking after cloth and cushions

Cloth is the most expensive part of a table and the easiest to ruin. The rule behind every maintenance job is the nap: worsted cloth has a direction, and brushing or ironing against it flattens the fibres permanently.

Brushing

  • Brush in the direction of the nap every time. Find the nap by running your hand along the cloth: one way feels slightly warmer and softer.
  • Use a brush with stiff bristles and a stiff brush for chalk marks. Soft brushes move dust but will not lift marks.
  • Around the pockets and along the cushions, brush from the pocket outwards.

Ironing

Use an iron on the cloth settings only, with a pressing cloth between the iron and the table so the heat does not scorch the fibres.

  1. Warm pass with a heavy iron.Work along the nap, in strips, moving the whole length of the table. Warmth sets the fibres in the right direction.
  2. Second heavy pass.This sets the direction properly and takes out the ripples from use.
  3. Finish with a light iron.This removes the shine and gives the nap an even finish.

Do not press hard enough to flatten the pile, and do not iron over the pockets. Full detail is in the table care guide.

Other care points

  • No water and no household cleaners.A damp cloth can shrink the cloth and leave a tide mark. Use a dedicated table cloth cleaner sparingly and follow its instructions.
  • Chalk marks:scrape the loose dust off first, then work the mark with a stiff brush along the nap. A spot-cleaner is the last resort.
  • Pockets:empty and vacuum them weekly. Balls that stay in a pocket put pressure on the cushion facing and on the cloth.
  • Balls:polish with a microfibre cloth and a ball cleaner only. Never use a wet cloth, which dulls the finish permanently.
  • Level and cloth tension:Check the table level monthly. An unlevel table makes the cue ball run off line and, eventually, stretches the cloth.
  • Re-cloth when the nap goes flat.Once cloth feels smooth under your hand and holds the cushion less reliably, plan a re-cloth rather than tightening screws.
On our tables. We brush the cloth along the nap before every session and schedule a full iron and pocket clean every month. Deep cleans are done overnight with the cushions lifted off. Cushion rubber degrades with age and is replaced rather than painted up.
