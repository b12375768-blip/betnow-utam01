Cloth, cushions and chalk: a table care routine

The felt on a table is the single most expensive part of it and the easiest to damage. Every routine in this guide is built around one idea: work with the direction of the nap, always.

Table maintenance

7 minute readWritten by our table maintenance staffUpdated 1 October 2026

“My table plays slow today” and “the cue ball does not run past where it should” are nearly always cloth problems, not cue problems. The balls on a worn or wrongly brushed table need more pace to travel the same distance, and the players behind the cue feel it as a difference in their own game.

Step one: find the nap

Worsted cloth is woven in one direction. With the nap, the fibres lie flat and the ball rolls fast and true; against it, the ball drags and can wander.

To find it, run your open palm along the cloth in both directions. The direction that feels warmer, softer and slightly more resistant is the nap. It is easier to find it once and then mark the same rail, because the nap runs from baulk cushion to top cushion on a snooker table.

Everything below assumes you brush and iron with the nap.

Step two: brush, every session

  • Use a stiff brush for chalk dust and marks; a soft brush only moves loose dust around.
  • Always stroke in the direction of the nap, from end to end.
  • Work around the cushions by brushing away from the pocket, not into it.
  • Empty and vacuum the pockets at the same time. Balls left in a pocket push on the cushion facing and eventually deform the pocket.

Brushing takes five minutes and is the highest-return five minutes on this list.

Step three: iron along the nap

An iron is for restoring the direction of the fibres, not for removing stains. Always put a pressing cloth between the iron and the table so heat cannot scorch the cloth, and use the table ironing setting, not maximum heat.

  1. Warm pass with a heavy iron.Work along the nap in strips, one strip at a time, keeping the iron moving and finishing at the end of the table.
  2. Second heavy pass.Same direction. This sets the fibres properly and takes out the ripple marks left by use.
  3. Finish with a light iron.This removes the shine and gives the whole table an even, matte finish.
  • Do not press hard enough to flatten the pile. You are grooming the cloth, not flattening it.
  • Do not iron across the cushions or over the pockets.
  • Do not iron against the nap, even in one small area. It leaves a visible stripe.

Step four: chalk marks

Chalk marks are normal; the wrong treatment is not.

  1. Scrape off loose dust with a plastic scraper or the edge of a triangle rack, taking care not to drag grit across the cloth.
  2. Work the remaining mark with a stiff brush, with the nap, using short firm strokes.
  3. If the mark remains, use a cloth spot-cleaner sparingly, following the instructions, and always test an out-of-the-way corner first.
Never use water.A damp cloth can shrink the cloth and leave a permanent tide mark around the edge. This is the most common way an amateurly clean ruins a good table.

Step five: cushions, pockets and level

  • Check the level monthly.A level table makes the cue ball run straight. Put a cue or a straightedge on the cloth with a ball touching it at both ends; if the ball drifts to one side, the table needs levelling.
  • Look at the cushion cloth.Fuzz or a slightly flattened line along the cushion nose means the cloth has been brushed across the cushion instead of away from it.
  • Check pocket plates and net strings.A rattling ball in a pocket is slow play for everyone.
  • Inspect the slate boltsif a table develops a distinct rise on one side. Tightening a slate is a job for an engineer, not for a member with a spanner.

Step six: the balls

  • Polish with a microfibre cloth. Marked cue balls are the most visible sign of a badly kept room.
  • Use a proper ball cleaner sparingly. A wet cloth dulls the finish permanently.
  • Wipe balls before they are put away, and never leave them on a windowsill or in direct sunlight.
  • Check the balls for chips and scuffs. A chipped ball runs unpredictably and marks the cloth.

When does a table need re-clothing?

Signs that cloth or cushions are finished
What you noticeWhat it means
The cloth feels smooth under your hand rather than fuzzyThe pile has been flattened and cannot be revived by ironing
The cue ball does not run past the spot on a rolling shotExcessively worn nap; braking effect is too high
Cushions have a rounded, hard edge and rebound is weakCushion rubber has degraded and needs replacing, not tightening
Sharks in the cloth near the cushion lineThe slate edge is moving; needs an engineer, not an iron
Repeated re-spotting of balls and slow roll on the last few feetCloth is stretched and slack; it can be re-stretched once, then it is time for new cloth

What we do on our tables

  • Brushed along the nap before each session.
  • Pockets emptied and vacuumed weekly.
  • A full iron and pocket clean once a month, with the cushions lifted for a deep clean.
  • Level checked monthly.
  • Cloth and cushions replaced on a planned cycle rather than after they have started playing badly.

Players can help

  • Chalk on the rail or on the cloth edge, not in the middle of the playing surface.
  • Report a rough spot, a lifting cloth edge or a rattling pocket to staff immediately.
  • Do not stand on a cushion to reach a ball. Use a rest.
  • Do not place a hot drink on the rail.
  • Return the balls to the rack area, not loose on the table.
